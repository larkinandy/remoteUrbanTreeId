01 / THE QUESTION

What can we learn about an urban tree from above?

A consistent view of urban forests starts with knowing which trees are there.

Trees differ in the shade they provide, their vulnerability to pests, and the pollen they release. But field inventories are costly to maintain, and cities record their trees in different ways.

This study asks whether public aerial imagery, satellite observations, and canopy-height data can help classify individual trees across U.S. cities.

The aim is to predict groups of trees that these observations can distinguish reliably. A group prediction is not a species identification or a direct estimate of ecosystem services.

02 / BUILDING A VIEW OF EACH TREE

One tree, several kinds of evidence

A photograph describes the canopy at one moment. Height measurements add structure. Repeated satellite observations show how vegetation changes through the seasons.

Example views of the same northern red oak in Denver.

Putting the evidence in the right place

Inventory coordinates can miss the actual crown. Records were linked to detected crowns within 5 m, and inputs were extracted around those crown centers. Records were screened for coordinate problems, missing imagery, insufficient vegetation, and incomplete height coverage.

Satellite and weather cells are larger than many individual crowns. They provide information about the surrounding vegetation and conditions as well as the focal tree.

03 / STAGE ONE · LEARNING THE GROUPS

Which trees look different to the model?

Botanical names do not always correspond to differences visible from above. The first model learns a numerical signature for each tree, using examples with known taxon labels.

A signature, not a photograph

The model combines the observations into 64 numbers. These numbers describe patterns learned from the training examples; they are not 64 named ecological traits.

A stable set of labels

04 / STAGE TWO · MAKING A PREDICTION

Learn the labels once. Then predict the group.

The first stage defines the task. The second stage learns to assign a tree to those fixed groups using its remote-sensing inputs. It does not need that tree’s species name as an input.

A broader inventory at the resolution the evidence supports.

Public remote-sensing data can help classify individual urban trees into empirically distinguishable groups across a large, diverse dataset. That offers a basis for more consistent characterization of urban tree composition where field inventories are incomplete.

The groups capture remote-sensing similarity. They should not be treated as interchangeable ecological functions, exact species labels, or direct estimates of shade, carbon, or pollen.

The main test split evaluates new records from represented cities. It does not, by itself, establish accuracy in a previously unseen city. Local validation and attention to input quality remain necessary.
